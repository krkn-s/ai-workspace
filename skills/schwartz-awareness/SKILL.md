# Schwartz Awareness

Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966. The book gives no page template. It gives a way to decide **what to say first**: what the prospect already knows, and how tired the market is of the claim.

Two scales, often confused. Keep them apart:

- **Awareness** is the prospect: what he knows about his problem, the available solutions, and you.
- **Sophistication** is the market: how many claims like yours it has already heard.

Awareness picks the opening and the length. Sophistication picks the promise or the mechanism. Answer both before writing a page.

## The five levels of awareness

From least to most aware. Each one: what he knows, where the copy starts, what it must carry.

1. **Unaware**: does not know he has the problem. A commercial promise reads as an ad and dies. Start with a scene, a story, an identification. Long copy, build the problem before the solution.
2. **Problem-aware**: feels the pain, not the cure. Name the problem better than he can, then explain why the obvious fixes fail. Start with the problem, keep the product out of the headline.
3. **Solution-aware**: knows solutions exist, not yours. Start with the desire or the result, then differentiate on the mechanism: how yours works differently, not what it promises.
4. **Product-aware**: knows you, hesitates. Start with proof, the offer, risk reversal, objections, comparison. Less education, more certainty.
5. **Most aware**: wants it, needs a reason to act now. Price, availability, date, bonus. One call to action, no pedagogy.

The rule behind all five: the headline starts exactly where the prospect stands. Aware of the product, start with the product. Aware of the desire only, start with the desire. Aware of the problem only, start with the problem. Miss by one level and a good page fails.

The job of the first line is not to sell. It is to make the second line read.

## The five stages of market sophistication

From virgin to exhausted. Each one: what the market has heard, what wins now.

1. **Virgin market**: nobody has made the claim. State it plainly. No cleverness, no mechanism.
2. **Claim copied**: competitors say the same thing. Enlarge the claim: bigger, faster, more specific.
3. **Claims exhausted**: nobody believes a bigger promise. Introduce a named mechanism and explain how it works.
4. **Mechanisms exhausted**: rivals have mechanisms too. Enlarge and detail yours: more proof it works, more of the process.
5. **Everything said**: nothing new can be claimed. Shift to identity, brand, and lived experience.

Pages often fail here. On a saturated market, "a site that converts" says little because everyone says it. A named mechanism is one way to regain attention and credibility.

## Reading the level from the traffic

- Precise query ("price of a website redesign"): product-aware or solution-aware.
- Broad query ("why is my site not converting"): problem-aware.
- Cold ad or cold list: unaware or problem-aware.
- Brand search, returning visitor: most aware.
- Retargeting: product-aware.

Prefer one dominant awareness level per page. Mixing levels can dilute the opening and the call to action. With mixed traffic, pick the dominant level and accept that other segments may be less directly served.

## Using it

- Audit a page: name the level it assumes, compare with the level the traffic actually is. The mismatch is the most common conversion bug, and a design review never sees it.
- Write a page: pick the level, then the opening, the length, and a single action.
- Compare two drafts: they often differ only by the level each one assumes.
- Do not raise a small business to level 1. Educating a market is expensive and pays back late. Sell to the awareness that already exists.
- Pick the container after the level. PAS, PASTOR, BAB, 4P and FAB are containers; the level decides which one and which first sentence. A wasted PASTOR at the wrong level reads worse than a plain PAS at the right one.

## Limits

- A practitioner's model from 1966, written for direct mail. No data behind it.
- It says nothing about the offer, the price, the proof, or the page speed. Those four can outweigh awareness, so test the model rather than treating it as law.
- It assumes homogeneous traffic. SEO, paid, and brand visitors on one page force a compromise.
- Labels vary across editions and summaries (need-aware, offer-aware, unaware). The logic is stable, the words are not.
